### adpll-vs-analog-pll.md

```markdown
---
title: "ADPLL vs analog PLL: choosing for your next node"
description: "A decision guide for choosing between an analog charge-pump PLL and an all-digital PLL: node, jitter target, spurs, area, portability and verification flow."
date: 2026-08-20
author: "[NAME: to be supplied]"
authorRole: "Technical lead, PLL/ADPLL and DDR PHY"
area: pll-adpll
tags: ["PLL", "ADPLL", "clocking", "jitter", "FinFET"]
readingMinutes: 8
---

The question arrives in every SoC planning meeting at an advanced node: do we keep the analog PLL we know, or move to an all-digital PLL? The honest answer depends on six things, and only one of them is phase noise.

## What each architecture is

An analog PLL compares reference and divided output in a phase-frequency detector, pumps charge into a passive loop filter, and tunes a voltage-controlled oscillator. The loop filter is a large capacitor. The VCO is an LC tank for low phase noise or a ring for small area and wide range. Fractional-N operation comes from a delta-sigma modulated divider.

An all-digital PLL replaces the detector and filter with a time-to-digital converter, or a single-bit bang-bang detector, feeding a digital loop filter. The oscillator becomes a digitally controlled oscillator: an LC tank with switched capacitor banks, or a ring with digital tuning. A delta-sigma dither on the fine capacitor bank smooths the frequency steps. The loop filter is synthesized logic. Only the TDC and the DCO remain analog.

## The six questions

### 1. Which node?

Below roughly 16 nm, the case for an ADPLL strengthens. Thin-oxide leakage makes a large analog loop-filter capacitor a liability. Charge-pump mismatch gets worse with lower supply headroom, and that mismatch is what makes reference spurs. Digital logic, meanwhile, is cheap and shrinks with every node. At 28 nm and above, a well-designed analog PLL remains compact and predictable, and the ADPLL's extra complexity buys less.

### 2. What jitter target, and where does it come from?

For a SerDes reference at [NUMBER: to be supplied] fs integrated jitter, the oscillator dominates. An LC DCO and an LC VCO in the same node achieve similar phase noise; the tank quality factor and the supply regulation matter more than which loop sits around it. The ADPLL adds a quantization noise floor from the TDC resolution that appears in-band. A fine TDC, or a bang-bang detector with the right loop gain, keeps it below the oscillator's contribution. If the budget is tight and the loop bandwidth is wide, the TDC becomes the design problem.

For a general-purpose SoC clock at a few picoseconds of jitter, either architecture is comfortable. Choose on area and portability.

### 3. What spur specification?

Analog PLL reference spurs come from charge-pump mismatch and leakage modulating the VCO control line. They are bounded by layout discipline and supply isolation and are hard to calibrate away. ADPLL spurs come from DCO quantization, dither tones and digital switching coupling into the oscillator. These are different problems. The digital ones respond to dither design and floorplanning; the analog ones respond to charge-pump design and loop-filter leakage control. Know which spur budget you have before choosing.

### 4. How much area?

The analog loop filter is usually the largest single item in an analog PLL at an advanced node. Moving it into logic removes it. The DCO capacitor banks add back some area, and the TDC is not free. Net, an ADPLL is typically smaller at FinFET nodes and roughly equal at planar nodes. [NUMBER: to be supplied] is a representative comparison from our own work, available under NDA.

### 5. Will this block move to another node?

An ADPLL's loop filter and calibration are RTL. They port with a synthesis run. The DCO and TDC are small analog blocks with clear interfaces. An analog PLL ports as a whole: charge pump, filter, VCO, bias and divider all need re-centering. If the roadmap has a second node within two years, the ADPLL's portability is worth real money.

### 6. Can your team verify it?

An ADPLL is a mixed-signal system. Its loop dynamics, lock behaviour and spur performance only show up when the RTL and the analog DCO run together. That needs a mixed-signal co-simulation flow, a behavioural model of the DCO that matches extraction, and engineers who are comfortable on both sides of the boundary. An analog PLL is verified with PSS/Pnoise and transient-noise simulation in one tool. If the team and flow are analog-only, the ADPLL's advantages can be eaten by a verification gap.

## The hybrid middle

Two designs sit between the extremes. A sub-sampling PLL keeps an analog loop but samples the VCO directly, removing the divider noise and lowering in-band noise. An analog PLL with digital calibration of the VCO band, loop bandwidth and charge-pump offset recovers much of the ADPLL's robustness without the TDC. Both are worth a look when the jitter target is aggressive and the node is planar.

## A decision table

| If your program has… | Lean towards |
| --- | --- |
| FinFET node, roadmap to a second node | ADPLL |
| Planar node, no port planned | Analog PLL |
| Sub-100 fs reference for SerDes, wide loop bandwidth | Either, with an LC oscillator; verify the TDC floor |
| Tight reference-spur budget, analog-only verification flow | Analog PLL with digital calibration |
| Area-constrained die at an advanced node | ADPLL |
| Many instances with different bandwidths | ADPLL (programmable filter) |

The right choice is usually clear once these six answers are on the table. The wrong choice is to decide by habit.
```

```markdown
---
title: "Porting a SerDes to a new node without losing a quarter"
description: "What changes when a transceiver moves between process nodes, where ports go wrong, and a plan that treats the port as a design project with a head start."
date: 2026-07-10
author: "[NAME: to be supplied]"
authorRole: "Technical lead, SerDes and high-speed I/O"
area: serdes

A SerDes port looks like the cheapest project on the roadmap. The architecture is proven, the schematics exist, the layout has been through silicon. Then the schedule slips by a quarter because the port was treated as a copy. Here is what changes, where ports fail and how to plan one so the quarter stays yours.

## What actually changes between nodes

**Devices.** Threshold voltages, transconductance efficiency and transit frequency all move. A slicer that had margin at the old node may not resolve the same input at the new one. Current-mode logic that was fast enough may now burn too much, or not be fast enough at the lower supply.

**Supply.** A step from, say, 0.9 V to 0.75 V removes headroom from every stacked structure: cascoded current sources, source-followers, CML buffers with tail currents. The bias scheme that worked becomes marginal at the slow corner.

**Metal stack.** Thin lower metals get more resistive; top metals change thickness and pitch. Inductors re-center. The clock distribution's RC changes, which changes skew between lanes and between TX and RX. Long signal routes that were fine now need re-buffering.

**ESD.** The protection targets stay the same; the device capacitance per unit of protection does not. Pad capacitance moves, and with it the bandwidth of the TX and RX termination network.

**Rules.** Density, double patterning, minimum-area and antenna rules all change. Layout that was clean cannot simply be scaled.

**Passives.** Metal-oxide-metal capacitors, resistors and varactors have different density, matching and voltage coefficients. The loop filter and the CTLE's RC network re-center.

## Where ports go wrong

- **Scaling the layout.** Geometric scaling ignores the metal stack, the rules and the ESD. It produces a layout that fails DRC and a clock tree with the wrong skew.
- **Copying bias ratios.** Current mirrors sized by ratio at the old supply saturate at the new one. Every bias point needs re-centering, not re-scaling.
- **Trusting the old jitter budget.** PLL phase noise, supply sensitivity and the CDR's tracking all move. Re-run the jitter budget with the new node's oscillator and buffers before touching schematics.
- **Forgetting the pads.** The termination network, ESD and bump or pad capacitance set the channel's first discontinuity. Re-simulate return loss with the new pad model, early.
- **Verifying at the end.** A port verified only after layout discovers the headroom problem when there is no time left to fix it.

## A plan that keeps the quarter

**Weeks 0 to [NUMBER: to be supplied]: gap analysis.** Characterize the new node's devices in the configurations the design uses. Re-run the link, jitter and power budgets with new-node models. Build a checklist of every block, with a verdict: reuse, re-center or re-architect. Deliver the checklist and a revised schedule. This phase is short and decides everything that follows.

**Weeks [NUMBER: to be supplied] to [NUMBER: to be supplied]: re-architect what the gap analysis flagged.** Typically the bias scheme, the clock distribution, the ESD and termination network, and sometimes the oscillator. These are small blocks with large consequences.

**Weeks [NUMBER: to be supplied] to [NUMBER: to be supplied]: re-center and re-layout.** Critical paths first: TX driver, CTLE, slicers, phase interpolators, clock buffers. Non-critical blocks are laid out in parallel. Every block is simulated post-layout at the worst corner before the next one starts.

**Final weeks: sign-off and the delta report.** Full post-layout extraction, DRC/LVS/antenna/density clean, and a delta report that puts old-node and new-node results side by side for every specification line. The delta report is what lets the integration team trust the port without re-reading the original verification.

## Re-use what you can

Testbenches, corner setups, measurement scripts and the compliance test plan port almost unchanged. So do the architecture document and the calibration algorithm. Reusing the verification infrastructure is where the real head start is, not in the layout.

## The one-line version

A port is a design project that starts with a proven architecture and a complete verification plan. Budget it like one, and the quarter stays on your roadmap.
```

```markdown
Teams that have shipped SerDes often assume a die-to-die PHY is the same job with an easier channel. The channel is easier. Almost everything else moves.

## What the specification actually hands you

UCIe splits a chiplet link into three layers. The protocol layer carries PCIe, CXL or a raw streaming mode. The die-to-die adapter handles link state, CRC and retry. The physical layer, which is what a PHY team owns, has two halves: the electrical analog front-end with its forwarded clock, and the logical PHY that runs training, lane repair, scrambling and the sideband.

Two package classes set the design envelope. In a standard package on an organic substrate, bump pitch is in the order of a hundred micrometres and a module is sixteen lanes wide. In an advanced package on a silicon bridge or interposer, bump pitch drops to the tens of micrometres and a module is sixty-four lanes wide. Per-lane data rates are defined in steps from a few GT/s to the low tens of GT/s. The raw bit-error-rate target is strict at the lower rates and relaxed at the top rates, where the adapter's CRC and retry are expected to carry the difference. Check the revision your program targets for the exact tables; they have changed between revisions and will again.

## It is a parallel bus with a forwarded clock

The first mental shift: a UCIe module is source-synchronous. Each module forwards a clock alongside its data lanes. There is no clock and data recovery per lane and, in the common configurations, no equalization. The receiver samples with a clock that travelled the same package route as the data.

That removes a CDR and a DFE from every lane. It adds a skew budget across sixteen or sixty-four lanes that the package, the bump map and the on-die routing all eat into. Per-lane deskew, duty-cycle correction on the forwarded clock and a clean clock-to-data training sequence become the critical analog items. The skew budget belongs on the first page of the architecture document, with the package team's name on it.

## The bump map is the floorplan

In a SerDes, the pad ring is a constraint. In a UCIe PHY, the bump map is the design. Shoreline bandwidth, the data rate you get per millimetre of die edge, is fixed by bump pitch, lanes per module and modules per edge. The analog front-end has to fit under its own bumps, with routing that keeps lane-to-lane skew inside the budget.

Practical consequences:

- ESD structures are sized to the package class. Advanced packages allow reduced charged-device-model targets, which means smaller pad capacitance and a lighter driver. Do not carry over the SerDes pad ring.
- Supply and ground bump assignment decides simultaneous-switching noise across a module. Simulate a full module switching, not one lane.
- The PHY's aspect ratio is dictated by the shoreline. Expect a long, thin block with the clock path running the length of it.

## Lane repair is part of the PHY

Advanced-package assembly yield is not perfect. The specification includes redundant lanes and a repair mechanism so that a module with a bad bump still trains. The repair multiplexers sit in the data path and add load to every lane. They have to be in the timing and skew budget from day one, and the repair logic has to be verified together with the analog front-end, not as a separate RTL block.

## Sideband is the control plane

A low-rate sideband interface, with its own forwarded clock and its own always-on supply domain, carries link initialization, parameter exchange and management traffic. It is simple electrically and demanding architecturally: it has to work before the main band is trained, during power states when the main band is off, and across dies that may have been designed by different teams. Treat it as a product in its own right with its own test plan.

## Verification without a compliance board

A SerDes gets a compliance board and a channel you can measure. A chiplet link only exists once two dies are assembled. Verification leans on package-extracted S-parameters, on full-module simulation with crosstalk and supply noise, and on interoperability planning with whoever builds the other die. If both dies are yours, plan a known-good-die test strategy early. If one is a partner's, agree the interop test matrix before tape-out, not after assembly.

## What to settle before schematics start

1. Package class, bump pitch and the bump map owner.
2. Modules per edge and the shoreline bandwidth target.
3. Data rate per lane and the BER target that goes with it.
4. Skew budget split between die and package.
5. Repair strategy and the assembly yield assumption behind it.
6. Sideband power domain and what must work with the main band off.
7. Who builds the other die, and how the two will be tested together.

A PHY team that has these seven answers in writing has done most of the architecture work. The circuit design that follows is demanding but familiar: drivers, receivers, a clean clock tree and a layout that respects the bump map.
```
